## Working notes

Xupeng Chen's stress-test model argues rapid AI adoption produces a demand deficiency because economic institutions remain anchored to human cognitive scarcity, and that the resulting financial-contagion risk is concentrated through top-quintile earners who drive most US consumption and are heavily exposed to trillions of dollars in private-credit and mortgage markets.

A distinct academic angle from the existing roi-evidence family, which covers enterprise-level ROI (MIT NANDA), labor-market effects (Humlum/Vestergaard), and aggregate productivity (Acemoglu/Jones); Chen's model is about a different, demand-side macro mechanism -- income-distribution exposure to AI-driven labor displacement transmitting into consumer-credit and mortgage-market fragility. Xupeng Chen's stress-test model argues that rapid AI adoption produces a "distribution-and-contract mismatch" in which AI-generated abundance coexists with demand deficiency because economic institutions remain anchored to human cognitive scarcity (acx-108); the model attributes disproportionate financial-contagion exposure to top-quintile earners, who drive 47-65% of U.S. consumption and face the highest AI exposure, transmitting risk into $2.5 trillion of global private credit and $13 trillion of mortgage markets (acx-119). Graded as observation, not argument: the claim is about what Chen's model says and predicts, checkable directly against the paper, not an independent adjudication of whether the mechanism is correct. Carries a supports edge into ai-capex-outpaces-realizable-revenue-bubble-risk: financial-contagion risk transmitted through consumer credit is exactly the kind of downstream-fragility finding that counts toward a bubble-risk reading, via a mechanism (demand-side, labor-market-driven) distinct from that argument's existing revenue-gap and financing-structure premises. atoms_against carries acx-151 (ChatGPT's adoption speed, unlike any prior technology, reaching over 900 million weekly users) as a genuine complication: extremely fast, broad consumer-level adoption is in some tension with a "demand deficiency" thesis, even though consumer usage volume and the income-distribution effects Chen's model targets are not strictly the same measure.
