## Working notes

Historical infrastructure buildout episodes such as the 1840s British Railway Mania and the late-1990s telecom fiber boom provide ambiguous precedent for the current AI capex debate: they show both that large ex-ante buildouts can later look rational and that manias can persist despite the availability of trustworthy warning signals.

Odlyzko's railway-mania study finds trustworthy quantitative measures existed at the time showing demand would not support expected returns, yet the technology's power and interested-party manipulation of perception induced a 'collective hallucination' anyway (acx-114), and separately that the mania's most influential critics were themselves deluded, which made their warnings counterproductive rather than restraining (acx-126). Against that, Hogendorn's fiber-boom study finds 'excessive entry' was concentrated in carrier swaps and leases, not physical construction, which looked far more moderate once double-counting from shared route-miles is excluded (acx-115) -- a finding that complicates the simple 'telecom boom = pure waste' reading many bubble comparisons lean on. Odlyzko's Napster study (acx-113) and his warning against the post-dot-com mood swing toward unjustified pessimism (acx-125) both caution against reading fast adoption, or a later crash, as proof of anything in either direction.
