In the mid-eighteenth century, the Comte de Buffon set out, across the many volumes of his Histoire Naturelle, a theory that the New World's climate was too cold and too wet to sustain animal life at Old World scale: American quadrupeds, on his account, ran smaller than their European counterparts, and species held in common between the two continents diminished once transplanted to America. It was not a passing remark. Buffon worked the claim through specific animals -- reading the New World's own peccary as a diminished descendant of the Old World hog, and ranking the tapir, the largest quadruped he granted to the Americas outright, no bigger than a donkey where the elephant held the equivalent rank across the Atlantic.
<!-- claims: bjd-buffon-asserts-american-degeneracy "no bigger than a donkey where the elephant held the equivalent rank" bound-at=2026-08-25 -->

Thomas Jefferson took the claim personally, and answered it methodically. Query VI of his Notes on the State of Virginia does not merely assert the opposite of Buffon; it takes Buffon's own comparative, weight-based method and re-runs it against named animals and named pounds, arguing that the single largest quadruped peculiar to America outweighs the entire column of species peculiar to Europe combined, and backing the claim with field numbers -- Connecticut and Rhode Island bullocks slaughtered at up to 2,500 pounds, an American bear entered in his own comparative table at nearly three times the weight of its European counterpart. Jefferson said outright that he took Buffon "for my ground work, because I think him the best informed of any naturalist who has ever written" -- which makes the rebuttal an argument on Buffon's own evidentiary terms, not a dismissal of them.
<!-- claims: bjd-jefferson-weight-tables-rebut-buffon "single largest quadruped peculiar to America outweighs the entire column of species peculiar to Europe combined" bound-at=2026-08-25 -->

Jefferson did not stop at the page. While serving as the United States' minister to France, he arranged to have a moose -- skin, bones, and horns, procured in New Hampshire at his request -- shipped across the Atlantic and presented to Buffon himself in Paris, on the first of October, 1787, along with the horns of caribou, elk, deer, and roebuck for comparison. He was careful about it, too: in the very letter presenting the specimens, he told Buffon plainly that the elk horns sent were "remarkeably small" and that he had seen ones five and six times heavier, rather than let an unrepresentative sample quietly do work it hadn't earned.
<!-- claims: bjd-jefferson-moose-shipment-episode "shipped across the Atlantic and presented to Buffon himself in Paris, on the first of October, 1787" bound-at=2026-08-25 -->

What happened next is where the story most often gets ahead of the evidence. The popular version has Buffon examining the moose, conceding the point, and promising to correct the record in his next volume -- and then dying before he could. Buffon did die within the year, in April 1788, and no revision of the degeneracy thesis ever appeared under his name. But chasing the promise itself back to a document turns up nothing from 1787 or 1788: the line usually quoted traces to Jefferson's own recollection of the exchange, recounted to Daniel Webster around 1824, some thirty-seven years after the fact. Whether that retelling is basically accurate or has sharpened with age is not something a search of the surviving record can settle either way; what a survey of that record can show is that no contemporaneous document does the settling for us.
<!-- claims: bjd-no-primary-retraction-confirmed "chasing the promise itself back to a document turns up nothing from 1787 or 1788" bound-at=2026-08-25 -->

Read together, the two halves of Jefferson's response -- the table and the moose -- are more than a naturalist's rebuttal. They are a diplomat's rebuttal, mounted by a minister who understood that "your climate produces smaller creatures, man included" was never only a claim about elk horns.
