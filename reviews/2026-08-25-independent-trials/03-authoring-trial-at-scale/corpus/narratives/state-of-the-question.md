Two claims sit at the center of this corpus, and neither one is settled by the evidence collected here. One holds that the current pace of AI infrastructure spending substantially exceeds what current and near-term realizable AI-specific revenue can justify, financed increasingly by debt and off-balance-sheet structures, and that this indicates meaningful bubble risk. The other holds that the same spending is economically sustainable because it is substantially backed by contracted, prepaid, and capacity-constrained real customer demand rather than speculative overbuilding. The two are recorded as direct conflicts-with counterparts of each other, and reading the corpus straight through, that is the honest state of it: both arguments are built from real, checkable atoms, and neither has run out of ground to stand on.

The sustainable-buildout case rests on three premises, each of which is itself well evidenced rather than asserted on faith. Remaining performance obligations at the major AI-infrastructure vendors grew sharply through 2026, and those figures are disclosed and audited, not analyst estimates. Data-center vacancy, PJM's capacity-auction shortfall, and AWS's own reports of unserved customer demand together read as capacity genuinely lagging demand rather than capacity sitting unused. And prepaid, customer-financed contract structures, whatever else they say about vendor capital needs, are at minimum a signal of buyer conviction, since a customer does not prepay for capacity it does not expect to use. Put together, these three premises are what make "the buildout is demand-backed" a defensible conclusion rather than a hope.

The bubble-risk case rests on a comparably solid set of premises, and they are not simply the mirror image of the first three. David Cahn's revenue-gap methodology, tracked across four years of his own escalating figures, and Ed Zitron's independent tally that five major companies spent over $560 billion on AI capex between 2024 and 2025 against roughly $35 billion in AI revenue, both point at the same fact from different angles: realized, AI-specific revenue remains small relative to the capital committed against it. That gap is being financed in a way that adds its own risk on top of the revenue question, through debt issuance running at a 973.7 percent year-over-year jump and off-balance-sheet lease and guarantee structures independent estimators put in the trillions. And RPO growth, real as it is, does not by itself establish that demand is diversified or monetizing, because a material share of the reported backlog and AI revenue traces back to a small number of related, deeply discounted, or contested counterparties.

The genuine crux, once both sides' premises are on the table, is narrower than "is there demand" or "is there spending." Both are obviously true. The crux is whether the recorded RPO growth is the kind of demand signal it is being read as, or whether its quality is compromised by concentration and circularity in ways that make the backlog a weaker guarantee of future revenue than its headline size suggests. The corpus records both readings as true at once rather than as a contradiction to resolve: RPO growth is real, disclosed, and audited, and RPO growth does not by itself establish monetizable, diversified demand. A reader who wants a single verdict will not find one here, and that is not a gap in the corpus's coverage; it is what the two claims' own conflicts-with relationship is there to say plainly.

Stepping back from the company-level evidence, the corpus's macro and historical material cuts against confident framing on either side rather than settling the question itself. Existing macroeconomic modeling implies a middle reading: the projected productivity gains from AI are too modest to support claims of imminent transformative growth, yet the buildout's own capital scale already represents a real, positive economic footprint, which is inconsistent with a reading that the investment is purely wasteful. And the historical analogies people reach for most often, the 1840s British Railway Mania and the late-1990s telecom fiber boom, turn out to be ambiguous precedent rather than a clean parallel for either camp: both episodes show that large ex-ante buildouts can later look rational, and both show that manias can persist despite the availability of trustworthy contemporaneous warning signals. Neither history nor macro modeling hands either side a clean verdict.

Even the corpus's own attempt to connect ROI scarcity to bubble risk runs into an unresolved counter-reading rather than a rebuttal. The argument that scarce measurable enterprise ROI favors a bubble reading over a normal early-adoption lag has to survive the Federal Reserve's own staff assessment, which frames the same 2026 picture as an economy reorganizing around AI with real, specific effects, alongside limited signs of broad-based transformation in aggregate data, consistent with the standard sequence of capability first, investment second, productivity effects third. These two readings of the identical ROI-scarcity evidence cannot both be the last word, and the corpus does not resolve which is right. That is the honest state of the evidence this survey of surveys and claims can offer: two well-built cases, a narrower and more specific crux than either side's rhetoric usually admits, and a macro and historical backdrop that refuses to break the tie.
