Set the bull and bear arguments aside for a moment and ask a narrower question: independent of which camp turns out to be right, what do the filings, the auctions, and the fab disclosures in this corpus actually establish as fact? Not what they imply, not what they are marshaled to support, just what happened, at what scale, disclosed by the party with the most direct knowledge of the number. This is that reading, and it turns out to be less settled than either camp's confident framing suggests, even before a single interpretive claim is added on top.

Start with the capital expenditure figure itself, because it is the number both camps treat as their starting point, and it is not one number. Aggregate 2026 capex among the five largest U.S. hyperscalers roughly doubled year-over-year, landing somewhere in a $660-700 billion range built from company filings plus two independent analyst aggregations that do not fully agree with each other or with a third, separate estimate. At the company level the picture fragments further: Microsoft's capex rose roughly 80 percent to $115.9 billion in property-and-equipment additions, while Oracle's capex and remaining performance obligations both grew several-fold in the same fiscal year even as its free cash flow turned sharply negative, a divergence Oracle attributes to its own cloud infrastructure investment. And once the aperture widens beyond the five headline hyperscalers to independent trackers, including Bain, Synergy Research, and Dell'Oro, the figures diverge substantially enough that no single settled "the capex number" exists even among specialist industry analysts working from public disclosures. This is not a bull or bear fact. It is a measurement fact: the buildout is large by any tracker's count, and its exact size depends on which tracker, which company set, and which time window you pick.

Move upstream to the supply chain and the figures get cleaner, because they are further from any single hyperscaler's own framing incentive. TSMC's Q2 2026 revenue rose 33.7 percent year-over-year with a 67.7 percent gross margin, and advanced nodes of seven nanometers and finer, the process generation AI accelerators actually use, accounted for 77 percent of total wafer revenue, not a broad-based semiconductor upcycle that happens to include AI. ASML raised its full-year 2026 sales guidance and is adding 30 percent to its lithography capacity for 2027; Samsung and SK hynix both posted record memory results on AI server demand, with SK hynix crossing 100 trillion won in first-half revenue for the first time in company history. Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue reached $10.8 billion for the quarter, up 143 percent year-over-year, extending the demand signal beyond NVIDIA GPUs specifically into custom accelerator and networking silicon. These are realized figures on income statements, several links removed from any hyperscaler's own capex announcement, and they are what money actually landing in a chipmaker's revenue looks like, whatever the eventual return to the hyperscalers deploying that hardware turns out to be.

The power picture is the sharpest figures-only finding in the corpus, because it is where a physical constraint, not a financing structure or a revenue projection, does the work. North American data-center vacancy has held at 1 percent for a third consecutive year even as more than 66 gigawatts of capacity, an electricity requirement exceeding Germany's, is under construction. PJM's 2028/2029 capacity auction cleared at the FERC-approved price cap and still fell short of the reliability requirement by 6,831 megawatts, the first RTO-wide shortfall in the grid operator's history, alongside the prior auction. AWS reports unserved customer demand even after adding 3.9 gigawatts of new power in 2025, and Microsoft carries an $80 billion backlog of unfulfillable Azure orders attributed specifically to power constraints. Together these three disclosures, from a real-estate data provider, a grid operator, and two hyperscalers' own reported experience, name electricity availability specifically, not chips and not real estate, as the constraint currently binding the buildout's pace. The honest complication sitting alongside this finding is that not every regional grid tells the same story at the same intensity: ERCOT's own approved-versus-observed data shows a large share of already-approved large loads not yet drawing anywhere near their approved capacity, which means the power constraint is real in at least PJM's footprint without being uniformly binding everywhere at once.

What these figures do not pin down, and what a figures-only reading has to say plainly rather than smuggle past, is return on capital. Record upstream semiconductor results confirm AI hardware is being purchased and installed at scale; they cannot by themselves establish that the hyperscalers deploying that hardware are earning an adequate return on the capital spent, because a chipmaker's revenue realizes at the point of sale regardless of what the buyer subsequently does with the silicon or whether it ever generates a positive return. The free-cash-flow figures make the same point from the buyer's side, and they diverge sharply by company rather than telling one story: Amazon's trailing free cash flow turned to an outflow, Oracle posted a fiscal-year free cash flow of negative $23.7 billion, and Alphabet reported a negative $5.9 billion quarter, all while capital expenditure accelerated, but Microsoft's free cash flow grew in the same period despite comparable capex growth. A single aggregate capex or revenue number would erase exactly this texture. The honest figures-only reading is that the physical buildout is real and large by every available measure, that upstream chip and power disclosures confirm demand is materializing rather than merely being announced, and that whether the capital spent on it is being earned back is a genuinely different question the same filings do not answer, and answer differently company by company where they come closest to touching it.
