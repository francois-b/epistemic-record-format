The skeptical case against the AI buildout is easy to caricature as pure vibes, a short-seller's grudge dressed up in big numbers. The corpus does not support that caricature. It supports something narrower and harder to dismiss: four separate lines of evidence, each with its own methodology and its own honest limitations, that converge on the same conclusion from different directions rather than repeating one analyst's opinion four times. This is that case stated as strongly as the corpus's own atoms allow, including the places where it is thinner than its proponents' rhetoric suggests.

Start with the revenue gap, because it is the most quantified of the four. David Cahn's methodology, tracked across four years of his own figures from a 2023 "$125B hole" to an entering-2026 statement that realized AI end revenue remains in the tens of billions annually against trillions in five-year infrastructure investment, and Ed Zitron's independent tally that five companies spent over $560 billion in AI capex between 2024 and 2025 against roughly $35 billion in AI revenue, do not simply repeat each other; they arrive at the same order-of-magnitude gap from different starting assumptions. Aswath Damodaran's valuation math sharpens the same point from a third angle entirely: at current aggregate market pricing for AI companies, the industry needs $5 trillion in annual revenue to break even on a ten-year horizon. None of these three is a raw disclosed figure. Each is one source's methodology applied to disclosed inputs, which is the honest caveat, and each is still standing after the corpus's own counter-evidence sweep: Microsoft's cloud revenue and Google's cloud backlog are real, but they are cloud revenue, not AI-specific revenue, and the gap these three sources describe is a gap in the latter, not the former.

The circular-financing thread is where the bear case stops being about arithmetic and starts being about structure. NVIDIA investing in or guaranteeing payment to customers who in turn buy NVIDIA hardware is not, on its own, proof that demand is fake; it is proof that some of the demand signal is not independent of the supplier reporting it, which is a different and in some ways more corrosive problem, because it means the corpus's own demand-signal claims cannot be taken at pure face value without checking which counterparty is actually paying. That structural concern connects directly to the RPO-quality argument: rapid backlog growth at AI-infrastructure vendors does not by itself establish that demand is diversified or monetizing, because a material share of reported backlog and AI revenue traces to a small number of related, deeply discounted, or contested counterparties. The honest complication here is real too. A first gigawatt of NVIDIA-OpenAI capacity is already targeted for delivery in the second half of 2026, and a twenty-year lease names OpenAI as the actual operating customer of real infrastructure, not a paper entity. The bear case's best version does not pretend these deployments aren't happening; it argues that their financing structure means the revenue backing them is not as independent, or as diversified, as the raw backlog number implies.

On enterprise return, the evidence is thinner than the other three threads, and an honest telling says so plainly rather than papering over it. The headline figure, that 95 percent of organizations report zero measurable return despite $30-40 billion in enterprise generative-AI investment, and the MIT Project NANDA diagnosis that the barrier is a learning failure rather than an infrastructure, talent, or regulatory one, both trace to a single study. Nothing else in this corpus independently corroborates that 95 percent figure; the closest adjacent evidence, a Danish labor-market study finding modest individual time-savings from chatbot adoption, measures a different thing entirely and neither confirms nor contradicts the enterprise-ROI finding on its own terms. The argument built on top of this evidence, that ROI scarcity favors a bubble reading over a normal early-adoption lag, is honest about facing a live, unresolved counter-reading from the Federal Reserve's own staff, who read the identical 2026 picture as an economy still early in the standard capability-then-investment-then-productivity sequence. The bear case's best day does not claim this argument is settled. It claims the evidence points its direction and states clearly that a single study is doing the load, and that a reasonable diffusion-lag reading has not been ruled out.

The depreciation thread is the newest and smallest of the four, but it is the one that goes after the buildout's accounting directly rather than its demand story. Michael Burry's argument that CoreWeave functions as an off-balance-sheet vehicle whose infrastructure depreciates faster than dot-com-era Level 3 Communications did is grounded in a real, fast-moving number: CoreWeave's own depreciation and amortization rose from $996 million to $2.5 billion in six months, a 2.5-times increase. That is a genuine data point, not an inference. What is inference is the further step from "CoreWeave's D&A is growing fast" to "AI hardware, generally, is being consumed faster than industry accounting assumes," and the corpus is honest that nobody has directly measured GPU economic life here; Burry is a named, interested short-seller reasoning from an aggregate company-level number, not a neutral appraiser of chip longevity. Set against this is Microsoft's own decision to extend the assumed useful life of its datacenter and office assets from 15 to 25 years, which mechanically slows depreciation recognition and raises reported near-term operating income even as cash capital expenditure keeps climbing. The bear case's best version does not claim these two facts are strictly the same asset class in contradiction; Microsoft's extension covers buildings, not GPUs specifically. It claims the two facts together are the right shape of worry: one company's own real-world hardware is aging faster than a comparison-era telecom bust, while another company is choosing, at the accounting level, to assume its infrastructure will last longer, in the same year, in the same industry.

Put the four threads together and the bear case's best version is not "there is no demand" or "the technology doesn't work." It is narrower and, for that reason, harder to argue away: that realized AI-specific revenue remains small relative to committed capital by several independent measurements, that a meaningful share of the demand signal backing that capital is not fully independent of the supplier reporting it, that the clearest evidence on enterprise return sits on one study rather than several, and that at least one infrastructure operator's own numbers are aging faster than the accounting treatment elsewhere in the same industry assumes. None of the four threads alone would carry the argument. Together, drawn from filings, an independent tally, a single but unrebutted enterprise study, and one company's own disclosed depreciation schedule, they are the strongest honest version of the case that this corpus supports, and the corpus is equally honest that the other side's premises, RPO growth is real, capacity is genuinely constrained, buyers are prepaying with their own capital, survive this case rather than being refuted by it.
