## Working notes

Existing macroeconomic modeling of AI's aggregate effects, using current task-exposure estimates, implies only modest total-factor-productivity gains over the next decade, well short of the transformative growth some AI proponents project.

Daron Acemoglu's model caps 10-year TFP gains at 0.66%, revised down to under 0.53% adjusting for harder-to-learn tasks (acx-106), and separately predicts AI widens the capital-labor income gap (acx-107); acx-130 records Acemoglu's own caution that his estimate may be exaggerated precisely because current productivity evidence comes from easy tasks, and future effects will come from harder ones with fewer objective outcome measures to learn from -- a hedge that cuts toward his number being an upper, not a central, bound. atoms_against carries the long-run alternative directly: Charles Jones's 'weak-link' framing, cited by name because it offers a genuinely different scenario rather than a quibble with Acemoglu's arithmetic -- if AI eventually automates away nearly all weak links, per-capita growth (stable at ~2%/year for 150 years) could exceed 5%/year (acx-133), on a horizon Jones himself measures in decades, not years (acx-109). The two are not strictly incompatible (near-term-modest, long-run-larger is a coherent combined view), but they pull in different directions on how much weight 'modest' should carry in the sustainability debate, which is why acx-133 sits in atoms_against rather than being folded into atoms_for as if the two economists agreed.
