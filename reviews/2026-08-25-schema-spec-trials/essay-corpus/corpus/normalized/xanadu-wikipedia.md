First hypertext project, founded in 1960

"Xanadu Project" redirects here. For other uses, see [Xanadu (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanadu_(disambiguation) "Xanadu (disambiguation)").

**Project Xanadu** ([/ˈzænəduː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*ZAN-ə-doo*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"))^([\[1\]](#cite_note-1)) was the first [hypertext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypertext "Hypertext") project, founded in 1960 by [Ted Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Nelson "Ted Nelson"). Administrators of Project Xanadu declared it superior to the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web "World Wide Web"), with the mission statement: "Today's popular software simulates paper. The World Wide Web (another imitation of paper) trivialises our original hypertext model with one-way ever-breaking links and no management of version or contents."^([\[2\]](#cite_note-2))

*[Wired](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wired_(magazine) "Wired (magazine)")* magazine published an article entitled "The Curse of Xanadu", calling Project Xanadu "the longest-running [vaporware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaporware "Vaporware") story in the history of the computer industry".^([\[3\]](#cite_note-WiredCurse-3)) The first attempt at implementation began in 1960, but it was not until 1998 that an incomplete implementation was released. A version described as "a working [deliverable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deliverable "Deliverable")", **OpenXanadu**, was made available in 2014.

## History

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Project_Xanadu&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")\]

Nelson's vision was for a "digital repository scheme for world-wide electronic publishing". Nelson states that the idea began in 1960, when he was a student at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"). He proposed a machine-language program which would store and display documents, together with the ability to perform edits. This was different from a [word processor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_processor "Word processor") (which had not been invented yet) in that the functionality would have included visual [comparisons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_comparison "Data comparison") of different versions of the document, a concept Nelson would later call "intercomparison".^([\[4\]](#cite_note-StruppaDechow2015-4))

On top of this basic idea, Nelson wanted to facilitate nonsequential writing, in which the reader could choose their own path through an electronic document. He built upon this idea in a paper to the [Association for Computing Machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Computing_Machinery "Association for Computing Machinery") (ACM) in 1965, calling the new idea "zippered lists". These zippered lists would allow [compound documents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_document "Compound document") to be formed from pieces of other documents, a concept named [transclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transclusion "Transclusion").^([\[5\]](#cite_note-5)[\[4\]](#cite_note-StruppaDechow2015-4)) In 1967, while working for [Harcourt, Brace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harcourt_Trade_Publishers "Harcourt Trade Publishers"), he named his project Xanadu, in honor of the poem "[Kubla Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kubla_Khan "Kubla Khan")" by [Samuel Taylor Coleridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Taylor_Coleridge "Samuel Taylor Coleridge").^([\[4\]](#cite_note-StruppaDechow2015-4))

Nelson's talk at the ACM predicted many of the features of today's hypertext systems, but at the time, his ideas had little impact. Though researchers were intrigued by his ideas, Nelson lacked the technical knowledge to demonstrate that the ideas could be implemented.^([\[3\]](#cite_note-WiredCurse-3))
