The Epistemic Record Format

ERF is a plain-text record format for working research: the analysis, argumentation and synthesis done in consulting, strategy, due diligence and organizational decision-making, where conclusions rest on claims nobody can later trace, check, or stand behind. Academic publishing has citations and peer review. Software has types, tests and version control. The knowledge work between them has had prose. ERF supplies what prose leaves out: evidence recorded verbatim and checkable against a frozen copy of its source, claims typed by what would settle them, arguments as typed relations between claims, and an append-only ledger of who stands behind each claim, since when, and why. LLMs make the records maintainable, because drafting, extracting and classifying is what they are good at. People make the judgments. The record shows which was which.

A working corpus of 151 atoms, 53 claims, 6 surveys and 71 sources, on whether AI infrastructure spending is backed by demand, is rendered here in the reference viewer: read the corpus. Zero conformance violations, every quote checked. The tour below is the fastest way in.

Six things that go wrong, and what the record does about each

None of these are new. What is new is that an LLM can now produce a month of plausible prose in an afternoon, so the cost of not fixing them compounds faster than it used to.

  • Plausible text arrives faster than anyone can check it. An LLM writes fluent, well-organized, correctly-hedged prose about anything, including things that are not so. Volume is no longer the constraint; knowing which sentences are backed is. The unit of evidence is an atom: one verbatim quote from one source, with one sentence saying what the quote shows. It is small enough to check by machine and small enough to check by eye.
  • Knowledge work has no version control and no tests. A codebase tells you what broke and when. A folder of documents tells you nothing: there is no build that fails, no diff that means anything, no test that a conclusion still follows from what it was built on. A corpus is checkable the way a codebase is. A validator reads it and reports violations, and the reference implementation runs as erf-check on the command line. What it cannot decide it names rather than guessing.
  • Provenance is lost between the reading and the writing. Somebody reads a report, writes a paragraph, and six months later the paragraph is the only survivor. The link is a URL that has moved, or a citation to a work nobody re-opens, and what the source actually said is gone. Every quote is checked against the source's normalized text, a copy the corpus holds, never against the live web. A dead link becomes weaker provenance rather than a broken check, and the check is re-runnable years later by anyone holding both.
  • Verdicts get stored as facts. A tool judges a source reliable, or a claim verified, and the judgment is written into a field. A year on, nothing says who judged it, under what procedure, or whether the thing judged has changed since. A judgment is recorded per auditor with the protocol version that produced it, so two verdicts under different procedures are never read as like for like. The mechanical part, whether the quote occurs in the text, is stored nowhere at all: it is recomputed on every read, because a stored result is a claim about a check rather than the check.
  • Nobody records who stands where. Disagreement in an organization lives in memory and in meetings. Who accepted a conclusion, when, on what grounds, and whether they later changed their mind are the facts most worth having and the ones least often written down. The standings ledger is append-only, one entry per person, humans only, each carrying a date, a stance and a reason. Nothing is ever edited or deleted; leaving a position is its own dated entry. A claim's disposition, whether it is a proposal, active, contested, rejected or retired, is computed from that ledger every time it is read and is never a stored field, so no strength of evidence can promote a claim nobody has committed to.
  • Staleness is invisible. The thinking underneath a document moves, and the document keeps saying what it said. Nothing marks which paragraphs depended on what changed. A passage of prose ends with a narrative binding: the claims it rests on, a few exact words from the passage so software can find it again after an edit, and the date the binding was made. When a bound claim moves, every passage resting on it can be listed. The same computation covers verdicts that predate what they judged.

A flag is not a violation

The six answers above produce two kinds of report, and the difference is the part most systems get wrong. A violation says the corpus does not conform. A flag says something here wants a person's attention, and a corpus carrying flags and no violations conforms perfectly well. Flags exist because several of the conditions this format cares about arise without anyone touching the record that carries them: withdrawing from a claim elsewhere hollows an argument that rested on it, and editing a claim ages every passage bound to it. Making either a violation would let one person's permitted act break another person's untouched corpus. So the format flags, and a consumer may neither present a flag as a failure nor hide one.

The tour

Four pages, in order. The corpus was written on 2026-08-25 by eleven LLM agents running in sequence, each reading what the last one left, none of them having seen anything of this project beyond the specification. It was brought to the current specification and checked on 2026-08-26.

  1. A claim with the evidence against it on the same page

    Look at the two evidence tables. The case against this claim is not filed as a rival claim somewhere else; it sits on the claim itself, under Evidence against, because evidence against a statement weakens its position and never changes its identity. Then look at the standings ledger at the bottom, which is empty, and at the disposition above, which computes to proposal because of that.

  2. A quote, checked

    The page runs the mechanical check and then shows you the source's held text with the quoted span marked inside it. The check is not a similarity score. It normalizes both sides through one published sequence, splits the quote on its elision markers, and requires every span to occur in order, on whole-word boundaries, without crossing a paragraph break the quote does not itself hold. This quote failed that last test until 2026-08-26, because it had run two paragraphs of the earnings call together into one sentence.

  3. A search recorded as evidence

    Read the yields column. Absence cannot be evidenced by a quote, because there is nothing to quote, so the format records the search itself: which instrument, which query, what scope, and what came back in the instrument's own words. This one is a deliberate re-run of an earlier survey, chained to it by prior_survey, because what a survey conducted is immutable and a search run again is a new record rather than an edit to the old one.

  4. Prose that names what it rests on

    Each paragraph ends with a line naming the claims it stands on. The bindings are invisible in the source document, spelled as HTML comments so they survive any markdown pipeline, and each carries a few exact words of its own passage so the binding still finds the paragraph after somebody rewrites the sentence around it. Follow one into a claim and back.

Two more pages are worth a minute. Corpus health is every computed flag in one place: 120 of the 151 atoms carry no audit verdict, and the page lists them by name rather than reporting a percentage. Sources is the whole source list, split by whether the text may travel: 69 of the 71 works ship their normalized text so the checks run here, and the two that do not each record why, because a validator can tell a recorded absence from an omission and cannot tell an omission from an oversight.

Status

Version 0.9, and numbered below 1.0 deliberately. It was extracted from a working practice rather than designed in advance: roughly 740 audited atoms and 300 claims across seven corpora came before the specification did. On 2026-08-25 ten independent trials read the document and nothing else, six of them building implementations in Python, Rust, SQL, Go, Haskell and Swift. They found a hole in the quote check that let a fabricated quotation pass, a requirement that could not be implemented as written, and two that contradicted each other. Every one was ruled and pinned with a conformance case the same day. What 1.0 waits on is implementations other than the author's, and feedback is invited.
