For most of the developed world, ensuring a healthy domestic market for strategic industries is usually the top priority, with import/export balances used as a valve to dampen the impact of economic cycles on local champions. As we have tirelessly pointed out over the years, this is simply not so for China, which regularly flips the script. When the goal is global domination, the domestic market is used as the toggle.

This play follows a familiar script. In the early stages, local champions are heavily subsidized on the home front until they have either developed or pilfered the necessary technology to compete on a global scale. Then the world is flooded with excellent products offered at impossible-to-compete-with prices, with the goal of quite literally putting the competition out of business. If this means the likes of Mr. Wang have to wait to get their shiny new vehicles, so be it. There's a greater national interest at stake, and Chinese leaders will pursue it with ruthless efficiency.

Were this just a story condemning the likes of General Motors, Ford, Toyota, Honda, and Volkswagen to ruin, it would be bad enough, but this exact sequence is playing out in the all-important artificial intelligence (AI) industry, only at an even faster pace. From cutting-edge semiconductor chips to frontier models, Beijing is tracing a familiar pattern, and we see no evidence that Western leaders properly understand what is happening, let alone are capable of doing much about it.
